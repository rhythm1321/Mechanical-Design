Surface Defect Detection Using U-Net

Developed and trained a U-Net convolutional neural network to perform pixel-level semantic segmentation of manufacturing defects on magnetic tile surfaces. The project combined image preprocessing, dataset management, convolutional neural networks, model training, performance evaluation, and qualitative analysis to investigate automated visual inspection for industrial manufacturing.

01 — The Problem

Automating Surface Defect Inspection

Surface inspection is an important part of manufacturing because defects can affect product quality, reliability, and performance. Traditional inspection methods can require significant manual effort and may become less consistent when defects are small, low contrast, or visually similar to the surrounding material.

Why Segmentation?

Classification can determine whether an image contains a defect, but it does not identify the exact position or shape of that defect. Semantic segmentation instead assigns every image pixel to either the defect or background class, making it more useful for visual inspection and downstream manufacturing decisions.

Project Objective

Train a neural network capable of taking a grayscale magnetic tile image as input and producing a binary mask identifying the location and shape of surface defects.

02 — Dataset

Magnetic Tile Surface Defects

The project used the Magnetic Tile Surface Defects dataset containing grayscale images organized into six categories. Five categories contained visible manufacturing defects, while the free category contained defect-free magnetic tile surfaces. Each defective image was paired with a ground-truth mask showing the exact defect region.

03 — Data Preparation

Preparing Images for Supervised Segmentation

Each defective category was split separately into approximately 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing data. This ensured that all five defect categories were represented across the model-development stages.

STEP 01

Load Images

Read magnetic tile images and their corresponding ground-truth masks.

STEP 02

Resize

Resize inputs to 256 × 256 pixels.

STEP 03

Normalize

Convert grayscale intensities from 0–255 to 0–1.

STEP 04

Binarize Masks

Defect pixels become 1 and background pixels become 0.

STEP 05

Batch Training

Shuffle image-mask pairs and process them in batches of eight.

Class Imbalance

The free category contained substantially more images than all five defective categories combined. Using all defect-free images during training could cause the network to overemphasize background pixels.

Balanced Training Strategy

The defective training set contained 274 image-mask pairs. Only 55 defect-free samples were added to training, approximately 20% of the number of defective training images. The remaining 897 free images were reserved for a separate false-positive evaluation.

04 — U-Net Architecture

Encoder-Decoder Segmentation Network

The model implemented for this project was a standard U-Net convolutional neural network. U-Net combines a contracting encoder path that learns image features with an expanding decoder path that reconstructs spatial information and produces a pixel-level segmentation mask.

Encoder

Learning Defect Features

The encoder contained four stages using 32, 64, 128, and 256 filters. Each convolutional block used two 3 × 3 convolutional layers followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. A 2 × 2 max-pooling layer reduced the image dimensions after each stage, allowing the network to learn increasingly abstract defect features.

Decoder

Recovering Spatial Detail

The decoder used transposed convolutions to increase feature-map dimensions. Decoder stages used 256, 128, 64, and 32 filters. At each level, the decoder features were concatenated with the corresponding encoder features using skip connections. These connections help preserve spatial details needed to locate small defects and identify defect boundaries.

05 — Training Configuration

Optimizing Pixel-Level Defect Detection

Training combined binary cross-entropy with Dice loss. Binary cross-entropy evaluated individual pixel predictions, while Dice loss encouraged overlap between the predicted segmentation mask and the ground-truth defect region.

Optimizer

Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001.

Batch Size

Training samples were processed in batches of eight image-mask pairs.

Training Length

Training was limited to a maximum of 30 epochs.

Loss Function

Combined binary cross-entropy and Dice loss.

Early Stopping

Configured to stop after seven epochs without improvement in validation Dice.

Learning Rate Reduction

Reduced by a factor of 0.5 after three epochs without validation-loss improvement.

06 — Training Performance

Monitoring Generalization Across 30 Epochs

Training continued for all 30 scheduled epochs. The highest validation Dice coefficient occurred during epoch 29, and the corresponding saved weights were restored for final testing. Training performance continued improving near the end of training while validation performance decreased slightly during the final epoch, indicating mild overfitting.

10 — Analysis & Findings

What the Model Learned — and Where It Struggled

The results show that the U-Net was capable of learning meaningful spatial information about manufacturing defects, but its segmentation quality depended strongly on defect appearance and size.

Precision vs Recall

Conservative Defect Predictions

Precision was substantially higher than recall. This means that when the network predicted a pixel as defective, that prediction was usually correct. However, the lower recall indicates that many ground-truth defect pixels were not detected, causing some predicted masks to capture only part of the true defect.

79.64%
Combined Test Precision
Best Overlap

Fray Produced the Highest Dice Score

Fray defects achieved the strongest overall overlap between predicted and ground-truth masks. The category produced a Dice coefficient of 0.7039 and IoU of 0.5430. Because the fray test set contained only five images, however, the category-level result is sensitive to individual samples.

Small Defects

Thin Cracks Were Difficult to Preserve

Crack defects produced comparatively low recall. One contributing factor may be the resizing of every image to 256 × 256 pixels. Thin cracks occupy relatively little image area and may lose important visual information when downsampled, making them more difficult for the network to segment completely.

11 — Future Development

Improving Segmentation Performance

The project successfully established a complete supervised segmentation pipeline, but several areas could be improved in future versions of the system.

Data Augmentation

Introduce controlled image augmentation to increase training diversity and reduce the effect of the relatively small labelled dataset.

Higher Image Resolution

Train using higher-resolution inputs to preserve thin cracks and small defect regions that may be lost after resizing to 256 × 256.

Attention-Based U-Net

Investigate attention mechanisms or more advanced U-Net variants to improve the model's ability to focus on subtle surface-defect features.

Larger Dataset

Additional labelled magnetic tile images would improve category balance and reduce sensitivity to individual validation and test samples.

Hyperparameter Exploration

Greater GPU availability would allow additional experiments involving learning rates, batch sizes, architectures, loss weighting, and training duration.

More Detailed Error Analysis

Evaluate false-positive predictions at the image level in addition to the pixel level to determine whether errors are concentrated in a small number of defect-free samples.
