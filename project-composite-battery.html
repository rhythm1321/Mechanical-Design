Graduate Engineering Project · Formula SAE EV

Composite Battery Enclosure Design & Structural Analysis

Evaluated fiber-reinforced composite materials for a Formula SAE electric-vehicle battery module enclosure, with the goal of replacing a conventional metallic structure with a lightweight, electrically insulating, and structurally robust laminate. The project combined material screening, Classical Laminate Theory, Tsai-Wu failure analysis, thermal expansion calculations, and SolidWorks finite element analysis to compare E-glass and Kevlar 49 designs.

01 — The Engineering Problem

Protecting High-Voltage Battery Cells Under Severe Vehicle Loads

Formula SAE electric vehicles package a large amount of stored energy into a compact accumulator. The battery module structure must prevent the cells from being mechanically damaged while also remaining electrically safe, lightweight, manufacturable, and practical for a student racing team. The design problem was therefore not simply to find the strongest material. The enclosure needed to balance structural performance with electrical insulation, mass, temperature exposure, cost, and fabrication requirements.

Structural Objective

Develop a composite laminate capable of supporting the FSAE acceleration load cases without exceeding the project limits for deflection or structural failure.

Material Objective

Compare electrically insulating composite systems and identify a material that provides sufficient strength and stiffness while minimizing enclosure mass.

03 — Material Screening

Narrowing the Candidate Set to E-Glass and Kevlar 49

E-glass, Kevlar 49, T700S carbon fiber, T300 carbon fiber, and carbon-Kevlar hybrid systems were considered. Carbon-fiber-based options offered excellent specific stiffness, but their electrical conductivity introduced additional insulation requirements around live battery components. E-glass and Kevlar were carried forward because both provide electrical non-conductivity while maintaining useful structural properties.

Candidate 01

E-Glass / Epoxy

E-glass provides a practical combination of mechanical strength, electrical insulation, environmental resistance, widespread availability, and relatively low material cost. Its primary drawback for this application is its higher density compared with Kevlar.

Electrically insulating Lower cost Composite density ≈ 2.186 g/cm³
Candidate 02

Kevlar 49 / Epoxy

Kevlar 49 combines electrical insulation with low density, high tensile strength, impact resistance, and strong resistance to crack propagation. These characteristics are well suited to the vibration and impact environment of a Formula SAE accumulator.

Electrically insulating Impact resistant Composite density ≈ 1.395 g/cm³
04 — Laminate Design & Classical Laminate Theory

Building Strength in Every Principal Loading Direction

A balanced and symmetric laminate was selected so that the panel could respond to longitudinal, lateral, bending, and shear loading while avoiding extension-bending coupling. The representative stacking sequence was [0 / +45 / −45 / 90]s. The 0° plies support the longitudinal direction, the 90° plies support the lateral direction, and the ±45° plies improve shear and multi-directional load transfer.

Classical Laminate Theory

Orthotropic lamina properties were transformed into the global laminate coordinate system and assembled into the laminate extensional, coupling, and bending stiffness matrices. Because the layup was symmetric, the coupling matrix was reduced to zero, simplifying the relationship between in-plane loading and bending.

{N, M} = [A B; B D] {ε⁰, κ}

The same framework was used to estimate laminate strains, stresses, effective stiffness, and the influence of thermal expansion.

Composite Shell Definition

Implementing the Laminate in SolidWorks

The panel was modeled as a composite shell with 0°, +45°, −45°, and 90° ply orientations. Each ply was assumed to be 0.125 mm thick, allowing the laminate to be increased in symmetric increments while preserving the selected stacking sequence.

05 — Analytical Sizing

Estimating Thickness, Failure Margin, and Thermal Response

Hand calculations were used as an initial design screen before finite element analysis. The direct longitudinal and lateral load cases required very little thickness based on simple allowable-stress calculations, while the vertical bending case controlled the preliminary plate thickness.

Preliminary Thickness

The analytical vertical-load estimate produced a required thickness of approximately 3.14 mm, which was conservatively rounded to a 4 mm, 32-ply laminate for the hand calculations.

Tsai-Wu Failure Analysis

Ply stresses were transformed into the local material axes and evaluated with the Tsai-Wu interactive failure criterion to estimate the laminate safety factor.

Thermal Expansion

A 50°C temperature change was evaluated. The calculated maximum in-plane expansion was approximately 0.414 mm along the panel length.

Analytical Deflection Check at 4 mm

Using equivalent laminate stiffness values and a simplified plate-strip approximation, the predicted maximum vertical deflection was approximately 0.391 mm for E-glass and 0.265 mm for Kevlar. These calculations indicated that both candidate materials could satisfy the 1 mm project deflection criterion.

06 — Finite Element Analysis Setup

Modeling the Actual Panel Geometry and Mounting Conditions

SolidWorks Simulation was used to refine the analytical estimates by including the real panel geometry, mounting holes, composite shell layup, orthotropic material properties, and the three required acceleration load directions. The mounting holes were constrained to represent the fastened interface between the battery module structure and its supporting structure.

Boundary Conditions

Fixture Definition

The fastener-hole regions were used as fixtures in the simulation. Including these local constraints was important because the FEA later showed that stress concentrations around the holes reduced the factor of safety compared with the simplified hand calculations.

07 — E-Glass Results

Iterating Thickness Until Both Design Criteria Were Met

E-glass was first evaluated under the 20 g vertical case because bending produced the most demanding deflection response. Increasing the laminate thickness reduced both deformation and failure risk. The final E-glass design required a 3 mm laminate to satisfy both the deflection and minimum factor-of-safety requirements before being checked under the 40 g lateral and longitudinal cases.

E-Glass Final Result

A minimum laminate thickness of 3 mm was established through the FEA iterations. At this thickness the design satisfied the project requirements in all three acceleration directions. Under the 40 g longitudinal case, the report identified a minimum Tsai-Wu factor of safety of approximately 3.5.

08 — Kevlar 49 Results

Achieving the Structural Targets at the Same Final Thickness

The Kevlar laminate followed the same iterative process. At 1 mm thickness, the 20 g vertical load produced approximately 4.5 mm of deflection. Increasing the laminate to 2 mm reduced deflection below the 1 mm requirement, but the factor of safety was still below the 1.2 target. Increasing the laminate to 3 mm produced a vertical factor of safety of approximately 2.6 and satisfied the remaining load cases.

Kevlar Final Result

A 3 mm Kevlar laminate met the project's deflection and factor-of-safety criteria in the vertical, lateral, and longitudinal FSAE load cases. The longitudinal result shown at the top of this page completes the three-direction validation of the final Kevlar configuration.

09 — Final Material Selection

Selecting Kevlar 49 for the Battery Enclosure

Both E-glass and Kevlar passed the structural requirements at a 3 mm laminate thickness, so the final decision was driven by system-level considerations rather than strength alone. Kevlar was selected because it provided comparable structural performance at a substantially lower composite density, while also offering electrical insulation, impact resistance, crack-arrest behavior, and vibration durability.

Mass Advantage

For the same 3 mm panel geometry, the report used composite densities of approximately 2.186 g/cm³ for E-glass and 1.395 g/cm³ for Kevlar. With both designs meeting the structural targets, the lower-density Kevlar laminate offered the stronger weight-saving opportunity.

Why Not Carbon Fiber?

Carbon fiber offered greater stiffness, but its electrical conductivity would require additional insulation between the enclosure and high-voltage components. That added complexity reduced its suitability for this specific battery-module application.

Final Selection: Kevlar 49 / Epoxy

The selected material system balanced the project priorities of mechanical performance, electrical safety, low mass, impact resistance, and manufacturability for a Formula SAE EV battery enclosure.

Electrically insulating Lower density than E-glass Impact resistant 3 mm laminate
10 — Manufacturing Strategy

Designing a Process Suitable for Low-Volume Student Manufacturing

Vacuum-assisted hand layup or vacuum-bag consolidation was selected as the recommended fabrication approach. The method provides a practical balance between laminate quality, tooling cost, and accessibility. Vacuum consolidation helps remove excess resin, reduce voids, and improve interlaminar bonding so the manufactured laminate more closely matches the assumptions used in the analytical and FEA models.

01

Prepare Tooling

Use a simple female mold with a smooth, sealed, release-treated surface.

02

Cut & Orient Plies

Cut the dry reinforcement and mark each 0°, ±45°, and 90° orientation.

03

Lay Up Laminate

Stack the plies in the selected symmetric sequence while wetting with epoxy resin.

04

Vacuum Consolidate

Apply peel ply, release film, flow media, vacuum bagging film, and vacuum pressure before cure.

Manufacturing Trade-Off

E-glass remains a strong lower-cost alternative and is easier to cut and finish. Kevlar requires more care during cutting and post-processing because aramid fibers tend to fray, but its lower density and impact-related advantages made it the preferred material for the final design recommendation.

11 — Key Outcomes

From Material Screening to a Validated Composite Design

The project established a complete analytical and computational workflow for evaluating composite battery-module structures. Rather than selecting a material using strength data alone, the final recommendation incorporated laminate mechanics, local FEA behavior, thermal response, electrical safety, weight, and realistic manufacturing constraints.

Engineering Takeaways

+ Converted FSAE acceleration rules into structural load cases
+ Developed orthotropic composite properties from constituent data
+ Applied Classical Laminate Theory and Tsai-Wu failure analysis
+ Compared analytical estimates against SolidWorks FEA
+ Identified mounting-hole stress concentrations as a key local effect

Final Design Recommendation

+ Material: Kevlar 49 / epoxy composite
+ Laminate thickness: 3 mm
+ Balanced symmetric 0° / ±45° / 90° architecture
+ Meets 20 g vertical and 40 g lateral / longitudinal load cases
+ Recommended process: vacuum-assisted composite layup
